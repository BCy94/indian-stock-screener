How to Read a Balance Sheet Like a Portfolio Manager

Published June 18, 2026 · by the So Called Investor desk

Most retail investors stop at the profit and loss statement. Revenue grew, margins held up, profit is up year-on-year — looks good, time to buy. The balance sheet, if it gets opened at all, gets a glance at the "total debt" line and nothing else.

That's a mistake. The P&L tells you what happened over a period. The balance sheet tells you what the business actually owns, owes, and how it's funded — the structural truth that determines whether last year's growth is repeatable or borrowed from the future.

1. Start with working capital, not debt

Before debt, look at receivables and inventory growth relative to revenue growth. If receivables are growing meaningfully faster than sales, the company may be stuffing the channel or extending credit to customers who can't pay on time. Both show up as "profit" today and a cash problem in twelve months.

A business can be profitable on paper and still run out of cash. Working capital is where that gap hides.

The quick check: compute days sales outstanding (DSO) and days inventory outstanding (DIO) for the last four years. A steady upward trend in either, without a clear operational explanation, is a flag — not a disqualifier, but a reason to read the notes to accounts before going further.

2. Separate productive debt from defensive debt

Debt itself is not the red flag — it's what funded it and whether the cash flows it generates can service it. A capital-intensive business (cement, power, telecom infrastructure) carrying leverage against long-life assets with contracted or predictable cash flows is a fundamentally different risk than a working-capital-starved business borrowing to stay afloat.

  • Interest coverage ratio — EBIT divided by interest expense. Below 3x deserves scrutiny in most sectors.
  • Net debt to EBITDA — above 3-4x outside of utilities/infra is usually a sign of an aggressively levered balance sheet.
  • Maturity profile — short-term debt funding long-term assets is a classic mismatch that blows up in tight credit cycles.

3. Read the contingent liabilities note — every time

This is the section almost nobody reads, and it's where guarantees, pending litigation, disputed tax demands and off-balance-sheet commitments live. None of it shows up in the headline numbers. A company with a clean-looking balance sheet and a contingent liabilities note larger than its net worth is not as clean as it looks.

4. Check what "other assets" and "other liabilities" are hiding

Catch-all line items are where unusual items get parked — capital advances to related parties, long-pending insurance claims, deferred tax assets that assume future profitability. A growing "other" bucket relative to the balance sheet size is a prompt to open the schedule, not a reason to panic on its own.

The five-minute version

If you only have five minutes: check the trend in receivable days, compute net debt to EBITDA, skim the contingent liabilities note, and compare cash flow from operations to net profit over the last three years. If operating cash flow consistently lags profit, the rest of this checklist just became mandatory reading.
